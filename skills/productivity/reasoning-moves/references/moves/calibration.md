# Judgment & calibration moves ("intuition," made teachable)

"Intuition" is not a sixth sibling domain alongside language, math, logic,
and philosophy — it's the trained *output* of the other four decks plus one
more skill: knowing how confident to be, and when to stop trusting your first
reaction. These moves make that checkable rather than a vibe. Mostly plug
into TRACE's Check and Express steps.

**Confidence tagging with justification.** State a confidence level (low /
medium / high, or a rough percentage) *and* the specific evidence-gap that
sets it — not a bare label. "Low confidence, because Check surfaced two
untested alternative explanations" is checkable; "low confidence" alone
isn't.
*Diagnostic case*: after working any TRACE case, ask "what exactly would
have to be true for your confidence to move from low to medium?"
*Common failure*: confidence tracking how the learner feels about the answer
rather than what Check actually found.

**Dual-process awareness (System 1 vs. System 2).** A fast, automatic
first-pass judgment (System 1) is often right but is exactly what produces
the tempting-wrong-answer pattern seen throughout these decks; a slower,
deliberate check (System 2) is what TRACE's Check step exists to force. The
skill is knowing *when* the fast answer is trustworthy (familiar, low-stakes,
well-practiced) and when it needs the slow check (novel, high-stakes,
involves a causal or statistical claim).
*Diagnostic case*: "A coworker's email reads only: 'Can we talk about the Q3
numbers when you have a minute?' Your gut says something's wrong. Should you
trust that gut read, or run TRACE's Check step first — and what about this
specific situation (stakes, ambiguity, how well you know this coworker's
normal tone) tells you which?" *Common failure*: applying the same trust
level to gut judgments regardless of stakes or familiarity — reading a
low-stakes, ambiguous message exactly like a high-stakes, unambiguous one.

**Named bias inventory.** A working set worth recognizing by name:
anchoring (over-weighting the first number seen), availability (judging
likelihood by how easily examples come to mind), confirmation bias (seeking
evidence that fits an existing belief), sunk-cost fallacy (continuing
because of past investment rather than future value), base-rate neglect (see
the quantitative deck), and the framing effect (the same facts read
differently depending on how they're phrased). Naming the specific bias in
play is what makes noticing it repeatable.
*Diagnostic case*: "A manager has spent six months and $200,000 building an
internal tool. A cheaper off-the-shelf option just became available that
would do the job better. The manager argues they should finish the internal
build since so much has already been invested." Which bias is driving this,
and what would an unbiased version of the decision look like? *Common
failure*: sensing "something's off" without being
able to name which bias, which makes the catch non-transferable.

**Reference-class forecasting.** Instead of estimating a novel case from
scratch, find a class of similar past cases and start from their typical
outcome, adjusting for what's actually different about this case. This
usually beats an inside-view estimate built purely from this case's own
specifics (planning fallacy: projects estimated from their own details alone
are reliably over-optimistic).
*Diagnostic case*: "You're estimating how long a new project will take by
listing its steps and adding up your best guess for each. What's a
reference-class estimate instead, and why might it be more accurate?"
*Common failure*: trusting a detailed inside-view estimate over a coarser
outside-view one because it feels more rigorous.

**Premortem.** Before committing to a conclusion or plan, ask "assume this
turned out to be wrong — what's the most likely reason?" This surfaces
objections that confirmation bias would otherwise filter out, because it
reframes the search as explaining a given failure rather than defending a
belief.
*Diagnostic case*: after the learner reaches a confident conclusion on any
case in this library, ask them to run a premortem on it before finalizing.
*Common failure*: treating the premortem as a formality and generating a
weak, easily-dismissed failure story instead of the most likely one.

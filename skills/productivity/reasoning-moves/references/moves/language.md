# Language moves (English / precision of word choice)

Moves for choosing and reading words precisely enough that they carry the
meaning intended, no more and no less. Mostly plug into TRACE's Record and
Express steps — precision at Record prevents smuggled inferences; precision
at Express prevents overclaiming.

**Definition hygiene.** Before arguing about whether something "counts,"
state what property makes it count. Most stuck disagreements are actually
disagreements about an undefined term, not the substantive question.
*Diagnostic case*: "Is a hot dog a sandwich?" — before answering, what
definition of "sandwich" is doing the work, and does the answer change under
a different reasonable definition? *Common failure*: arguing the object-level
question while never stating the definition being used, so the disagreement
never resolves.

**Ambiguity vs. vagueness.** Ambiguous: a word has two distinct meanings and
the sentence doesn't say which ("bank" as riverbank or financial bank).
Vague: a word has one meaning with unclear boundaries ("tall," "a lot").
They need different fixes — ambiguity needs disambiguation, vagueness needs a
threshold or a range.
*Diagnostic case*: "The report says the project is 'mostly done.'" — is
"mostly" ambiguous or vague, and what one question resolves it?
*Common failure*: treating a vague term as if picking the "right" meaning
(as with ambiguity) would fix it, when what's actually missing is a
threshold.

**Denotation vs. connotation.** The literal reference of a word (denotation)
and its emotional or evaluative coloring (connotation) can diverge —
"frugal" and "cheap" both denote spending little money, with opposite
evaluative coloring (virtue vs. vice). Notice when a word choice is doing
persuasive work that the facts alone don't support.
*Diagnostic case*: a colleague describes the same person as "frugal" in one
meeting and "cheap" in another, with no new facts between the two. What
changed, and which word is doing argumentative work the underlying facts
don't justify on their own? *Common failure*: treating connotation-loaded
language as neutral because it denotes the same underlying behavior.

**Calibrated hedging verbs.** "Suggests," "is consistent with," "indicates,"
"establishes," and "proves" are not interchangeable — each claims a
different strength of evidential support. Picking the wrong one overclaims or
underclaims what Check actually found.
*Diagnostic case*: "Three of the five customers who churned last month had
also filed a support ticket in their final week." Which verb correctly
describes what this fact does to the claim "poor support causes churn" —
*proves*, *establishes*, *suggests*, *is consistent with*, or *is unrelated
to*? *Common failure*: reaching for the strongest available verb (*proves*,
*establishes*) because it sounds more confident, independent of what the
evidence — a small sample, no comparison group — actually supports.

**Presupposition and framing.** A question can presuppose its own answer
("why did the change fail?" presupposes it failed) or a framing can smuggle
a value judgment into what looks like a neutral description ("tax relief"
vs. "tax cut"). Read for what's being assumed before answering what's being
asked.
*Diagnostic case*: "How do we stop the decline in employee morale?" — what
does this question presuppose, and is that presupposition actually
established? *Common failure*: answering the presupposed question directly
instead of first checking whether the premise holds.

**Gricean pragmatics (implicature).** What a sentence literally says and
what it conversationally implies can differ — "some of the team finished"
implies (without stating) "not all," because a cooperative speaker would have
said "all" if true. This implicature is *cancellable*: "some, in fact all"
is not self-contradictory the way a stated fact's negation would be — the
implication is a defeasible inference from context, not part of the literal
meaning. Notice when you're reading an implicature as if it were a stated
fact, or when you're relying on one without stating it.
*Diagnostic case*: "Some of the tests passed." — what does this literally
claim, what does it implicate, and could both be true while the implicature
is false? *Common failure*: recording the implicature at TRACE's Record step
as if it were a given fact.

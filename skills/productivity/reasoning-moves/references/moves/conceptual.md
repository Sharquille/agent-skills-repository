# Conceptual moves (Philosophy / drawing distinctions)

Moves for handling concepts precisely — what makes something count as an
instance of a category, and how to test a claimed definition or argument.
Mostly plug into TRACE's Apply step.

**Necessary vs. sufficient conditions.** A condition is necessary if the
thing can't hold without it; sufficient if the condition alone guarantees
the thing holds. Most definitional confusion comes from treating a necessary
condition as if it were sufficient, or vice versa.
*Diagnostic case*: "Being unmarried is required to be a bachelor." Is that
necessary, sufficient, both, or neither for something to count as a
bachelor? What's a counterexample either way? *Common failure*: conflating
"required" with "enough" — unmarried is necessary (no married bachelors) but
not sufficient (a 5-year-old boy is unmarried but isn't a bachelor either).

**Genus-differentia conceptual analysis.** Define a term by naming the
broader category it belongs to (genus) and what distinguishes it from other
members of that category (differentia) — "a square is a rectangle
[genus] with equal sides [differentia]." A definition missing either half is
incomplete.
*Diagnostic case*: define "bribery" this way — what's the genus (what
broader category of act is it), and what's the differentia (what makes it
bribery specifically rather than any other member of that category)?
*Common failure*: giving examples of the term instead of its genus and
differentia.

**Counterexample method.** To test a proposed definition or general claim,
look for a case that satisfies the definition but seems intuitively wrong
(or vice versa). One clean counterexample is stronger evidence against a
definition than many confirming examples are for it.
*Diagnostic case*: "Knowledge is justified true belief." Find a case where
someone has a justified true belief that doesn't intuitively count as
knowledge (a Gettier-style case) — what does that reveal about the
definition? *Common failure*: treating a counterexample as a minor exception
rather than evidence the definition itself needs revision.

**Steelmanning.** Before arguing against a position, state its strongest
defensible version — not the easiest version to knock down. A rebuttal that
only defeats a weak version of the opposing view hasn't shown anything about
the actual view.
*Diagnostic case*: take a position the learner disagrees with and ask them
to state its strongest form before responding to it. *Common failure*:
substituting a weaker, easier-to-refute version of the argument (a straw
man) without noticing the substitution.

**Category vs. instance (type vs. token).** A claim about a category (types)
and a claim about a particular member of it (tokens) are different claims —
"dogs are loyal" (a generalization about the type) doesn't entail "this dog
is loyal" (a claim about a token), and a counterexample token doesn't refute
a statistical claim about the type.
*Diagnostic case*: "Dogs are loyal, but my neighbor's dog just ran off with
a stranger — so the claim is false." Is that a fair refutation? Why or why
not? *Common failure*: treating a single counter-token as refuting a
general, non-universal claim about the type.

**Fact-value (is-ought) distinction.** On the standard (Humean) formulation,
a purely descriptive claim about what is the case does not by itself
establish a normative claim about what ought to be done — bridging them
needs an extra value premise, often left unstated and undefended. This is
itself a philosophical position, not a universally settled one (some
ethical naturalists argue certain facts carry normative weight directly),
but asking "what's the unstated value premise?" is useful regardless of
which side you land on.
*Diagnostic case*: "Most people do X, so X is the right thing to do." — what
unstated value premise would make this valid, and is that premise
defensible on its own? *Common failure*: treating the descriptive premise
(what most people do) as if it settled the normative question by itself.
